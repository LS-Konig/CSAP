\setstretch{2}
We distinguish between \textbf{explicit identifiers}, \textbf{implicit
partisans (vote-based)}, and \textbf{non-partisans}.

The core claim of the paper is that \emph{observed levels and
cross-national patterns of affective polarization (AP) depend
systematically on how partisanship and polarization are conceptualized
and measured} in multi-party systems.

\section{Data and empirical domain}\label{data-and-empirical-domain}

We use data from Hahm, Hilpert, and König
(\citeproc{ref-hahm2024divided}{2024}), collected in \textbf{2019} in 25
European democracies (all EU member states at the time except Luxembourg
and Malta, plus the UK).

The dataset has been used to study partyism and coalition partnership
dynamics (e.g., APSR article based on the same project), but our
contribution differs in focus:\\
we center on \textbf{conceptualization, measurement, and comparative
scope conditions of affective polarization} rather than coalition
alignment or generalized partyism.

Temporal limitation:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Data stem from a two cross-sectional waves (2019).\\
\item
  Findings do not capture post-2019 developments (e.g., pandemic
  politics, recent electoral realignments).
\end{itemize}

\section{Framing: Conceptual proliferation in comparative AP
research}\label{framing-conceptual-proliferation-in-comparative-ap-research}

Affective polarization has become central in political science and is
increasingly studied in \textbf{comparative multi-party systems}. This
expansion raises conceptual and methodological questions.

Recent work distinguishes between:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Ideological vs.~ideational polarization}
\item
  \textbf{Identity-based vs.~attitude-based affective polarization}
\item
  \textbf{Targets of affect}
  (\citeproc{ref-areal2024verticalvshorizontal}{Areal and Harteveld
  2024}; \citeproc{ref-rollicke2023polarisation}{Röllicke 2023};
  \citeproc{ref-reiljan2024patterns}{Reiljan et al. 2024}):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Vertical/political (toward political elites (LAP) / parties (PAP))
  \item
    Horizontal/social (toward fellow citizens / partisans)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

In multi-party systems, additional choices matter:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Ingroup identification}: explicit identification vs.~vote
  intention/choice\\
\item
  \textbf{Outgroup construction}: all parties vs.~most distant party\\
\item
  \textbf{Weighting by party size}\\
\item
  Inclusion (or exclusion) of \textbf{non-partisans}
\end{itemize}

Comparative research has rarely examined how these design choices
affect:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the magnitude of AP,
\item
  cross-country rankings,
\item
  and substantive inferences about democratic implications.
\end{itemize}

This is our entry point.

\section{Analytical separation of
components}\label{analytical-separation-of-components}

We proceed in three conceptually distinct steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Thermometer-based AP (attitudinal; vertical;
  party-targeted)}\\
  → Used to illustrate how operationalization choices affect comparative
  inference in multi-party systems.
\item
  \textbf{Behavioral AP (conjoint; horizontal; citizen-targeted)}\\
  → Estimated via solidarity and trust games.\\
  → Presented as behavioral consequences of partisan boundaries.
\item
  \textbf{Comparative scope and composition}\\
  → How the distribution of partisan types across 25 democracies
  conditions aggregate AP.
\end{enumerate}

We explicitly separate thermometer and game results to avoid conflating:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  concept,
\item
  measurement,
\item
  and behavioral manifestation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Part I -- Thermometer-based affective
polarization}\label{part-i-thermometer-based-affective-polarization}

This section uses \emph{only party-targeted thermometer items}.

Key limitation:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  No direct measure of affect toward fellow citizens.
\item
  Thus: vertical (party) rather than horizontal (citizen) AP.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Roadmap for this section}\label{roadmap-for-this-section}

We demonstrate how AP estimates change depending on:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Explicit vs.~implicit ingroup definition\\
\item
  Inclusion vs.~exclusion of non-partisans\\
\item
  Alternative aggregation and weighting strategies
\end{itemize}

Main goal: Show that in multi-party systems, the \emph{choice of
operationalization} meaningfully affects:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  effect sizes,
\item
  country rankings,
\item
  dispersion across countries.
\end{itemize}

This directly addresses open questions about the transferability of
U.S.-centric AP concepts to fragmented party systems.

\subsection{Comparative composition of partisan types (idea
notes)}\label{comparative-composition-of-partisan-types-idea-notes}

The share of:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  explicit identifiers,
\item
  implicit (vote-based) partisans,
\item
  non-partisans,
\end{itemize}

varies substantially across countries.

Loose idea for theoretical anchoring:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Link variation to classic work on partisan dealignment in Europe
\item
  Consider long-term differences in party system institutionalization
  and partisan attachment
\item
  Connect to literature on party identification decline and electoral
  volatility in Europe.
\end{itemize}

Potential implication: Countries with weak partisan identities may
mechanically display lower identity-based AP but not necessarily lower
behavioral discrimination.

This section remains exploratory and descriptive but could situate our
compositional findings in broader comparative political culture debates.

\section{Part II -- Behavioral polarization as
consequences}\label{part-ii-behavioral-polarization-as-consequences}

We present the conjoint results separately from thermometer analyses.

Conceptual shift:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  From \textbf{attitudinal differentiation toward parties (vertical)}\\
  to\\
\item
  \textbf{Behavioral discrimination toward fellow citizens
  (horizontal)}.
\end{itemize}

Outcomes:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Solidarity allocations (dictator game logic)
\item
  Trust allocations (trust game logic)
\end{itemize}

Here, affective polarization manifests as:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Differential allocation toward co-partisans vs.~out-partisans.
\end{itemize}

We treat these as \textbf{behavioral consequences of partisan
boundaries}, rather than as alternative scaling of thermometer AP.

Open conceptual question: To what extent do attitudinal (vertical) and
behavioral (horizontal) AP align within and across countries?

Possible empirical extension:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Estimate correlation between country-level attitudinal AP and
  behavioral AP (posterior correlation).
\end{itemize}

\section{Muslimhood, partisan interaction, and sorting (targeted
extension)}\label{muslimhood-partisan-interaction-and-sorting-targeted-extension}

The religion attribute in the conjoint is \textbf{uniformly randomized}
across:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Muslim\\
\item
  Christian\\
\item
  Non-religious
\end{itemize}

Thus, estimates assume \textbf{no religious sorting} in exposure.

Exploratory results (note to be formalized):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Muslim co-partisans receive fewer tokens than Christian or
  non-religious co-partisans.
\item
  Muslim out-partisans receive fewer tokens than Christian or
  non-religious out-partisans.
\item
  The penalty is larger within the co-partisan condition than within the
  out-partisan condition.
\item
  Interaction effects are therefore \textbf{asymmetric}, not uniform
  across partisan categories.
\end{itemize}

Two implications:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Theoretical}:\\
  One should not assume symmetric sorting or interaction effects between
  partisan and religious identities.\\
  Minority attributes may condition in-group favoritism differently than
  out-group derogation.
\item
  \textbf{External validity}:\\
  Because religion is uniformly distributed by design, real-world
  exposure patterns (where Muslim citizens are not evenly distributed
  across partisan categories) are not reflected.
\end{enumerate}

Possible extension:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Incorporate prior information about realistic joint distributions of
  religion × partisanship to simulate more externally valid scenarios.
\item
  Use the uniform design as a benchmark (``no-sorting counterfactual'')
  against which realistic sorting patterns could be contrasted.
\end{itemize}

system compositions and then averaged across 25 European countries. To
ensure comparability, attitudinal thermometer scores (originally 0--100)
have been rescaled to a 0--10 range to match the behavioral token scale.
Explicit partisans are those reporting a psychological attachment to a
party; Implicit partisans are those denying attachment but reporting a
vote choice; Total represents all individuals with a partisan anchor;

Error bars indicate 95\% confidence intervals based on cross-national
variance.}

Planned approach:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Hierarchical (partial pooling) estimation for country-level
  quantities.
\item
  EU-level benchmarks derived via marginalization over country-level

\item
  Report standardized or scale-relative effects (thermometer vs.~token

\section{Limitations (draft notes)}\label{limitations-draft-notes}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Party-targeted thermometers only}\\
  → No direct citizen-targeted affect measure in the attitudinal
  section.
\item
  \textbf{Weighting by party size}\\
  Assumes encounter probability proportional to electoral share.\\
  Social visibility may violate this (e.g., radical challenger parties).
\item
  \textbf{Uniform conjoint distribution}\\
  No endogenous religious sorting; real-world joint distributions may
  differ.
\item
  \textbf{Cross-sectional design (2019)}\\
  Results capture a specific pre-pandemic political context.\\
  No dynamic inference about temporal change.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contribution (working
formulation)}\label{contribution-working-formulation}

In comparative multi-party systems:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Affective polarization is not a single quantity.
\item
  Its magnitude and cross-national configuration depend on:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    ingroup definition,
  \item
    inclusion of non-partisans,
  \item
    aggregation rules,
  \item
    and whether polarization is conceptualized vertically or
    horizontally.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Rather than asking only \emph{``How polarized are European
democracies?''},\\
we ask:

\textbf{How does conceptualization reshape what, and even who, we
observe as polarized in multi-party contexts?}

\subsection{Experimental setup}\label{experimental-setup}

Before the behavioral games, Hahm, Hilpert, and König
(\citeproc{ref-hahm2024divided}{2024}) presented respondents a short
background information overview and instructions. For the dictator game,
these were: \emph{This game is played by pairs of individuals.}
\emph{Each pair is made up of a Player 1 and a Player 2.} \emph{Each
player will have some information about the other player, but you will
not be told who the other players are during or after the experiment.}
\emph{The game is conducted as follows: A sum of 10 tokens will be
provisionally allocated to Player 1 at the start of each round.}
\emph{Player 1 will then decide how much of the 10 tokens to offer to
Player 2.} \emph{Player 1 could give some, all, or none of the 10
tokens.} \emph{Player 1 keeps all tokens not given to Player 2.}
\emph{Player 2 gets to keep all the tokens Player 1 offers.} \emph{You
will play this game three times with three different people.} In the
trust game, the provided information and instruction were: \emph{This
game is played by pairs of individuals.} \emph{Each pair is made up of a
Player 1 and a Player 2.} \emph{Each player will have some information
about the other player, but you will not be told who the other players
are during or after the experiment.} \emph{Each player will receive 10
tokens.} \emph{Player 1 then has the opportunity to give a portion of
his or her 10 tokens to Player 2.} \emph{Player 1 could give some, all,
or none of the 10 tokens.} \emph{Whatever amount Player 1 decides to
give to Player 2 will be tripled before it is passed on to Player 2.}
\emph{Player 2 then has the option of returning any portion of this
tripled amount to Player 1.} \emph{Then, the game is over.} \emph{Player
1 receives whatever he or she keeps from the original 10 tokens, plus
anything returned to him or her by Player 2. Player 2 receives their
original 10 tokens, plus whatever he or she keeps after returning any
portion of the tripled amount to Player 1.} \emph{You will play this
game three times, with three different people.} \emph{The more tokens
you obtain, the more successful you will be.}

In both games respondents were shown a tabular overview of Player 2
after the instructions. Figure~\ref{fig-example-profile} shows an
example of such a profile along with the interface respondents were
provided to assign the 10 tokens. Each round, a new profile was
displayed to respondents.
