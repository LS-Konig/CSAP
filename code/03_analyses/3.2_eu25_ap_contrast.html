eu25games 2019: within-party AP contrast of explicit and vote-anchored partisans (model set-up)

Published

October 2, 2026

Setup

This notebook sets up the models for the first eu25games estimates. The quantity of interest is the gap in affective polarization (AP) between explicit partisans (attachment and vote name the same party) and vote-anchored partisans (no attachment, but a reported vote), estimated within party and country. In the composition figure of notebook 3.1, these are the dark-blue leaf (“Attached: same party”) and the dark-orange leaf (“No attachment: vote”). There are two outcomes, and each gets its own models:

  • Games: tokens (0–10) given to a partner in the dictator and trust games (horizontal, behavioral);
  • Thermometer: 0–100 ratings of parties (vertical, attitudinal).

The notebook follows the Bayesian workflow in eight steps: scientific model, estimand, data summary, formal model, prior predictive check, fit, MCMC diagnostics and posterior predictive check, estimand computation, limitations. For every outcome, a Gaussian model (on the standardized outcome) is built first, then a beta-binomial model. The beta-binomial is the generative model of the integer outcome. The Gaussian model recovers conditional means and serves as robustness.

Nothing in this notebook is a result. With full_fit <- FALSE (the default), every model is compiled and sampled on a 10% development subsample with short chains, only to show that the pipeline runs end to end: priors, prior predictive checks, sampling, diagnostics, posterior predictive checks and the estimand code. The estimates from these runs are not interpretable and are not interpreted. Setting full_fit <- TRUE switches every model to the full analysis sample and production settings, and caches the fits in data/04_models/ (git-ignored).

Run Settings

Two switches govern the whole notebook.

  • full_fit: FALSE fits every model on the development subsample (dev), with 2 chains of 300 iterations (150 warm-up). TRUE fits on the full analysis sample with 4 chains of 2,000 iterations (1,000 warm-up) and writes the fit to data/04_models/. A cached fit is reused as long as model, data and priors are unchanged (file_refit = "on_change").
  • seed: one seed for the development subsample, the samplers and the posterior draws used in the estimand code.

The sampler runs on cmdstanr (CmdStan 2.39). cmdstanr is not on CRAN; it comes from the Stan r-universe (install.packages("cmdstanr", repos = "https://stan-dev.r-universe.dev")). The rstan backend is not used, because the R 4.6 library pairs rstan 2.32 with StanHeaders 2.39, and that pair compiles no model. adapt_delta = 0.95 is set from the start, because hierarchical models with varying slopes at two levels tend to produce divergent transitions at the default of 0.8. The maximum tree depth stays at the default of 10, and the diagnostics count the iterations that hit it. When CmdStan compiles a model, the C++ compiler emits warnings about the generated Stan code (e.g. -Woverloaded-virtual). These say nothing about the model or the sampling, and fit_model() mutes exactly those messages. Every other brms message and every warning stays visible. Prior predictive fits always use the development subsample: they do not condition on the outcome, so a larger sample adds nothing.

Scientific Model

Before any model is chosen, the data-generating process is written down as a DAG. The DAG states what each part of the model is there for, and it says which reading of the contrast the design supports.

The nodes:

  • \(C\): country; \(P\): the respondent’s anchor party (the attachment party for explicit partisans, the vote party for vote-anchored partisans);
  • \(T\): partisan type (explicit or vote-anchored);
  • \(R\): the relation of the target to the anchor (co-partisan or out-partisan);
  • \(W\): survey wave (2 or 3). The vote item differs between waves (in wave 2 the European Parliament vote was mostly an intended or hypothetical one), so the wave can shift who ends up vote-anchored, and it can shift the outcome;
  • \(U\): unobserved respondent traits that drive both whether people report an attachment and how they treat partisans (political interest, strength of preference, social desirability, and so on);
  • \(Y\): the outcome (tokens or rating).

For the games there are three more nodes, all fixed by the design: \(Z\), the shown partner attributes (age, sex, religion, class, EU stance shown or not); \(G\), the game (dictator or trust); and \(K\), the round. \(Z\) is randomized. \(G\) and \(K\) are design factors. All three only affect \(Y\).

The arrow \(T \to R\) in the games is a design arrow. The partner’s party was drawn with reference to the attachment answer, so explicit partisans meet a co-partisan in about half of the rounds and vote-anchored partisans in about one in ten (notebook 3.1, How the Partner’s Party Was Drawn). Within a type, \(R\) is random. This is why AP is always computed within type: conditioning on \(T\) closes the only back door into \(R\).

The first call returns nothing: with \(U\) in the graph, there is no adjustment set that identifies the effect of type. (An empty set {} would instead mean that no adjustment is needed.) Without \(U\), the anchor party and the wave would suffice (\(\{P, W\}\)). For the co/out relation in the games, conditioning on type is enough (\(\{T\}\)).

The models below follow from this. The anchor party enters as a varying-effects level (nested in country), and the wave enters as a dummy. The quantity of interest, the type difference in the co/out gap, is an interaction \(T \times R\). A DAG does not encode interactions, but the gap itself is identified within type, and the comparison of gaps across types inherits the problem of \(U\). The type contrast is therefore a within-party descriptive contrast, not a causal effect. Under which assumptions it can be read causally is an open section in the notes (design.md). Respondent covariates that could stand in for \(U\) are not joined yet (notebook 3.1, Caveats).

Estimand

For a type \(t \in \{\text{explicit}, \text{vote-anchored}\}\):

\[ AP_t = E(Y \mid Co, t) - E(Y \mid Out, t) \]

\[ \Delta AP = AP_{\text{explicit}} - AP_{\text{vote-anchored}} \]

The expectations are marginal means over parties, so they need a stated average. They are computed by g-computation from the fitted model:

  1. Take the observed rows of the anchor parties in the estimand population (below).
  2. Set type and relation counterfactually to each of the four cells (explicit/vote-anchored × co/out), keeping everything else as observed, and predict the expected outcome for every row with all varying effects (country, party, person) included.
  3. Average the predictions with respondent weights: every respondent-wave counts once, whatever their number of rows. Each anchor party then weighs by its number of respondents of both types together. The weight is the same in all four cells, so \(\Delta AP\) is not driven by how types are distributed across parties.
  4. Take differences draw by draw.

Common support. The type contrast within a party exists only where the party has respondents of both types. The models are fitted on all anchor parties: single-type parties still inform the country and grand means through partial pooling. The estimands are averaged only over anchor parties with at least one explicit and at least one vote-anchored respondent. The data sections tabulate per country what falls outside.

The estimands are reported pooled (all parties in support), per country and per anchor party. They are reported in raw units (tokens, thermometer points) and in SD units: the raw contrast divided by the SD of the outcome in its analysis sample. The SD units make the attitudinal and behavioral contrast comparable. The cross-outcome quantity is

\[ \Delta AP^{SD}_{\text{thermo}} - \Delta AP^{SD}_{\text{games}}, \]

reported with the posterior probability that it is positive. The two models are fitted separately, so draws are paired by index, which is valid for independent posteriors. This quantity is coded here, but not interpreted.

The estimand code is shared by all models:

  • ap_draws() returns posterior draws of the four cell means per group.
  • ap_contrasts() turns them into \(AP_t\) and \(\Delta AP\).
  • summarise_ap() gives the posterior median and the 95% interval.

posterior_epred() is called in row chunks, so that the full samples fit into memory.

Code 
# Cell means per group, by draw
ap_draws <- function(
  fit,
  data,
  by = NULL,
  ndraws = 1000,
  back = identity,
  chunk = 5000
) {
  set.seed(seed)
  ids <- sample(seq_len(ndraws(fit)), min(ndraws, ndraws(fit)))
  grp <- factor(if (is.null(by)) "pooled" else data[[by]])

  # Respondent weights per group
  m <- sapply(levels(grp), \(l) (grp == l) * data$der_w)
  m <- matrix(m, nrow = nrow(data), dimnames = list(NULL, levels(grp)))
  rows <- split(seq_len(nrow(data)), ceiling(seq_len(nrow(data)) / chunk))

  cells <- expand_grid(der_explicit = 0:1, der_co = 0:1)
  map2(cells$der_explicit, cells$der_co, \(e, k) {
    nd <- mutate(data, der_explicit = e, der_co = k)
    tot <- 0
    for (i in rows) {
      ep <- back(posterior_epred(fit, newdata = nd[i, ], draw_ids = ids))
      tot <- tot + ep %*% m[i, , drop = FALSE]
    }
    tot <- sweep(tot, 2, colSums(m), "/")
    as_tibble(tot) |>
      mutate(.draw = ids) |>
      pivot_longer(-.draw, names_to = "group", values_to = "mean") |>
      mutate(der_explicit = e, der_co = k)
  }) |>
    bind_rows()
}

# AP by type and Delta AP, by draw
ap_contrasts <- function(draws, sd_y) {
  draws |>
    pivot_wider(
      names_from = c(der_explicit, der_co),
      values_from = mean,
      names_prefix = "m"
    ) |>
    transmute(
      .draw,
      group,
      ap_vote_anchored = m0_1 - m0_0,
      ap_explicit = m1_1 - m1_0,
      delta_ap = ap_explicit - ap_vote_anchored,
      delta_ap_sd = delta_ap / sd_y
    )
}

# Median and 95% interval
summarise_ap <- function(contrasts) {
  contrasts |>
    pivot_longer(-c(.draw, group), names_to = "quantity") |>
    summarise(
      median = median(value),
      lower = quantile(value, 0.025),
      upper = quantile(value, 0.975),
      .by = c(group, quantity)
    )
}

The MCMC diagnostics are also shared. mcmc_check() reports the largest \(\hat{R}\), the smallest bulk and tail ESS over all parameters, the number of divergent transitions, and the number of iterations that hit the maximum tree depth. One posterior warning is muted: “The ESS has been capped to avoid unstable estimates”. It fires once per parameter whose ESS estimate exceeds what the number of draws can support. With thousands of person intercepts it would otherwise flood the output, and it never signals a sampling problem. All other warnings stay visible.

Code 
mcmc_check <- function(fit) {
  # Mute only the ESS-cap note
  s <- withCallingHandlers(
    summarise_draws(as_draws_df(fit), "rhat", "ess_bulk", "ess_tail"),
    warning = \(w) {
      if (grepl("ESS has been capped", conditionMessage(w))) {
        invokeRestart("muffleWarning")
      }
    }
  )
  np <- nuts_params(fit)
  tibble(
    max_rhat = max(s$rhat, na.rm = TRUE),
    min_ess_bulk = min(s$ess_bulk, na.rm = TRUE),
    min_ess_tail = min(s$ess_tail, na.rm = TRUE),
    divergent = sum(np$Value[np$Parameter == "divergent__"]),
    max_treedepth = sum(
      np$Value[np$Parameter == "treedepth__"] >= max_treedepth
    )
  )
}

# Grouped PPC with an outside grouping variable
ppc_by <- function(fit, type, group, newdata, ndraws = 100, stat = NULL) {
  stopifnot(nrow(newdata) == nrow(fit$data))
  y <- as.vector(get_y(fit))
  yrep <- posterior_predict(fit, ndraws = ndraws)
  g <- newdata[[group]]
  switch(
    type,
    stat_grouped = ppc_stat_grouped(y, yrep, group = g, stat = stat),
    bars_grouped = ppc_bars_grouped(y, yrep, group = g)
  )
}

Games

The games sample keeps:

  • explicit and vote-anchored respondents (type);
  • rounds in which the partner’s party was shown and is a co- or out-partisan (der_cj_partisan_relationship). This drops conditions 3 and 4 (party not shown) and the Latvian partners shown “without a party”.

Both games and both waves are kept.

The model variables:

  • der_explicit (1 explicit, 0 vote-anchored), der_co (1 co-partisan, 0 out-partisan), der_trust (1 trust game, 0 dictator game), der_wave3 (1 wave 3, 0 wave 2): binary 0/1 dummies, not centred.
  • round (1–3 within game) and the shown partner attributes cj_age, cj_sex, cj_rel, cj_class and der_cj_eu_identity, all as factors, as is standard for conjoint attributes. Age is shown at five values and is treated as five levels, not as a number. There are no continuous predictors, so no predictor needs standardizing.
  • cj_nationality_shown is not used. When the party is shown (conditions 1 and 2), nationality is forced to the respondent’s own country, so it is constant here. The check below asserts this.
  • der_y_z: the token allocation z-scored on the mean and SD of this sample, the outcome of the Gaussian model.
  • der_w: the respondent weight of the estimand, one over the respondent-wave’s number of rows.
  • der_anchor: the anchor party with its country. Only used for tables; the model nests anchor in country.

Formal Models

All four models share one structure. For observation \(i\) of person \(j\), whose anchor party \(p\) lies in country \(c\):

\[ \eta_i = \alpha + \beta_R R_i + \beta_T T_i + \beta_{RT} R_i T_i + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{z}_i^\top (\mathbf{u}_c + \mathbf{u}_{cp}) + v_j, \]

where:

  • \(R_i\) is der_co and \(T_i\) is der_explicit;
  • \(\mathbf{x}_i\) are the controls (games: game, round, wave and the shown partner attributes; thermometer: wave);
  • \(\mathbf{z}_i = (1, R_i, T_i, R_i T_i)\);
  • \(\mathbf{u}_c \sim \text{MVN}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma_C)\) and \(\mathbf{u}_{cp} \sim \text{MVN}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma_P)\) are the country and party deviations in intercept, co/out gap, type gap and their interaction;
  • \(v_j \sim N(0, \sigma_v)\) is a person intercept that spans both waves of a panelist.

The party-level slopes let the type × relation contrast differ between parties, so it is estimated within party. Countries differ through \(\mathbf{u}_c\) and through the parties they hold. The game and the round are controls only: they shift the level, and they do not vary by party or country.

The two families:

  • Beta-binomial (the generative model): \(Y_i \sim \text{BetaBinomial}(n, \mu_i, \phi)\) with \(\text{logit}(\mu_i) = \eta_i\), \(n = 10\) tokens or \(n = 100\) thermometer points. \(E(Y_i) = n\mu_i\), and \(\phi\) governs how much the person-specific propensity spreads beyond the binomial. A small \(\phi\) allows the spikes at the scale ends.
  • Gaussian (robustness): \(Y^z_i \sim N(\eta_i, \sigma)\) on the z-scored outcome. It recovers conditional means, but it puts predictive mass outside the scale and cannot produce the spikes.

Priors

The priors aim for two things:

  • every coefficient is centred on zero, so that the prior expectation of every contrast is no difference;
  • the implied outcomes spread across the logical range of the scale rather than piling up anywhere.

The prior predictive checks below test the second aim.

Why these priors (history). A first set had wider priors: Intercept normal(0, 1.5), every b normal(0, 1), every SD exponential(1), and phi exponential(0.1); for the Gaussian, b normal(0, 0.5), SD exponential(2) and sigma exponential(1). It produced U-shaped prior predictive distributions, rendered in commit c48c1aa. In the games, the prior median share was 30% at 0 tokens and 26% at 10. In the Gaussian models, about 41–46% of the draws fell outside the scale. The cause is additive. On the logit scale, the predictor of one row sums the intercept, up to 17 control dummies, four varying effects at both the country and the party level, and a person intercept. With wide priors on each, the sum spreads over many logits, and the inverse logit then pushes most of the mass to the bounds. A small phi (mass near 0 under exponential(0.1)) adds a second U-shape inside every row.

The current set was chosen by simulating the prior predictive through the model’s own design (same dummies, centred intercept, varying effects at country, party and person level) on the development subsample. It keeps every coefficient centred on zero. It narrows the parts that only add spread (controls, varying-effect SDs, phi near 0), but not the three focal terms: co, explicit, and co × explicit. These keep a wider normal(0, 1). The prior must not rule out a large co/out gap. On the thermometer, a gap of 2–3 logits means, for example, 85 against 35 points: a large difference, but one the scale is built to record. A tighter focal prior would put such a gap many prior SDs out and pull the contrast of interest towards zero.

In brms, the Intercept class is the intercept at the sample means of the predictors, so its prior centres the average prediction.

  • Beta-binomial (logit scale).
    • Intercept normal(0, 0.75): centred on \(\mu = 0.5\) (5 tokens, 50 points), 95% of the mass between about 0.19 and 0.81.
    • Focal b normal(0, 1): a gap of \(\pm 2\) logits (0.5 to 0.12 or 0.88) remains well within the prior.
    • Control b normal(0, 0.25): game, round, wave and the shown partner attributes shift the level by at most about half a logit each.
    • sd exponential(5): mean 0.2 logit for every varying effect, with 5% of the mass above 0.6.
    • cor lkj(2): mild shrinkage of correlations towards zero.
    • phi gamma(4, 0.4): mean 10, SD 5. Less than 2% of the mass lies below 2, the region in which a beta distribution with mean 0.5 turns U-shaped. This rules out a U-shape by prior, but not by data: if the allocations call for a small phi, the posterior can still get there.
  • Gaussian (z-scale).
    • Intercept normal(0, 0.5).
    • Focal b normal(0, 1): a gap of up to about 2 SD remains within the prior.
    • Control b normal(0, 0.2).
    • sd exponential(5): mean 0.2 SD.
    • sigma exponential(2): mean 0.5 SD.
    • cor lkj(2).

In the simulation, the beta-binomial prior predictive is nearly flat across the whole range: each token value takes 8.5–9.2%, with 10% at 0 and 9.5% at 10. The Gaussian prior predictive puts a median of 16% of draws outside the scale. Some of that is unavoidable for a model that ignores the bounds.

Limitations and Open Points

  • The contrast is descriptive. Unobserved traits (\(U\)) can drive both reporting an attachment and treating partisans differently. No respondent covariates are joined yet, and the conditions for a causal reading are open (design.md).
  • Thin vote-anchored co-partisan cells in the games. Vote-anchored respondents met a co-partisan only by chance, about one round in ten, so many parties have few or no such rounds. Their party-level gap rests on partial pooling, and the intervals show it.
  • Out-partners differ by type. Explicit partisans’ out-partners were drawn from the other parties only, while vote-anchored respondents’ partners came from all main parties. Both are “out”, but from slightly different sets.
  • The Gaussian models are robustness. They recover conditional means, but they put predictive mass outside the scales and cannot produce the spikes at the ends.
  • The vote is a European Parliament vote, in wave 2 mostly an intended or hypothetical one (notebook 3.1).
  • Question order is unverified. Whether the thermometers came before or after the games is not established (notebook 3.1).
  • The priors on the varying-effect SDs are fairly tight (exponential(5), mean 0.2 on the logit or z scale). They were tightened to remove the U-shape of the first prior set (see Priors). With 25 countries and about 400 parties the data should dominate, but whether the posterior SDs press against the prior is worth checking after the full fit.

# Setup

This notebook sets up the models for the first eu25games estimates.
The quantity of interest is the gap in affective polarization (AP) between **explicit** partisans (attachment and vote name the same party) and **vote-anchored** partisans (no attachment, but a reported vote), estimated *within* party and country.
In the composition figure of notebook 3.1, these are the dark-blue leaf ("Attached: same party") and the dark-orange leaf ("No attachment: vote").
There are two outcomes, and each gets its own models:

- **Games**: tokens (0--10) given to a partner in the dictator and trust games (horizontal, behavioral);
- **Thermometer**: 0--100 ratings of parties (vertical, attitudinal).

The notebook follows the Bayesian workflow in eight steps: scientific model, estimand, data summary, formal model, prior predictive check, fit, MCMC diagnostics and posterior predictive check, estimand computation, limitations.
For every outcome, a Gaussian model (on the standardized outcome) is built first, then a beta-binomial model.
The beta-binomial is the generative model of the integer outcome. The Gaussian model recovers conditional means and serves as robustness.

**Nothing in this notebook is a result.**
With `full_fit <- FALSE` (the default), every model is compiled and sampled on a 10% development subsample with short chains, only to show that the pipeline runs end to end: priors, prior predictive checks, sampling, diagnostics, posterior predictive checks and the estimand code.
The estimates from these runs are not interpretable and are not interpreted.
Setting `full_fit <- TRUE` switches every model to the full analysis sample and production settings, and caches the fits in `data/04_models/` (git-ignored).

## Run Settings

Two switches govern the whole notebook.

- `full_fit`: `FALSE` fits every model on the development subsample (`dev`), with 2 chains of 300 iterations (150 warm-up). `TRUE` fits on the full analysis sample with 4 chains of 2,000 iterations (1,000 warm-up) and writes the fit to `data/04_models/`. A cached fit is reused as long as model, data and priors are unchanged (`file_refit = "on_change"`).
- `seed`: one seed for the development subsample, the samplers and the posterior draws used in the estimand code.

The sampler runs on `cmdstanr` (CmdStan 2.39).
`cmdstanr` is not on CRAN; it comes from the Stan r-universe (`install.packages("cmdstanr", repos = "https://stan-dev.r-universe.dev")`).
The `rstan` backend is not used, because the R 4.6 library pairs rstan 2.32 with StanHeaders 2.39, and that pair compiles no model.
`adapt_delta = 0.95` is set from the start, because hierarchical models with varying slopes at two levels tend to produce divergent transitions at the default of 0.8.
The maximum tree depth stays at the default of 10, and the diagnostics count the iterations that hit it.

Prior predictive fits always use the development subsample: they do not condition on the outcome, so a larger sample adds nothing.

# Scientific Model

Before any model is chosen, the data-generating process is written down as a DAG.
The DAG states what each part of the model is there for, and it says which reading of the contrast the design supports.

The nodes:

- $C$: country; $P$: the respondent's anchor party (the attachment party for explicit partisans, the vote party for vote-anchored partisans);
- $T$: partisan type (explicit or vote-anchored);
- $R$: the relation of the target to the anchor (co-partisan or out-partisan);
- $W$: survey wave (2 or 3). The vote item differs between waves (in wave 2 the European Parliament vote was mostly an intended or hypothetical one), so the wave can shift who ends up vote-anchored, and it can shift the outcome;
- $U$: unobserved respondent traits that drive both whether people report an attachment and how they treat partisans (political interest, strength of preference, social desirability, and so on);
- $Y$: the outcome (tokens or rating).

For the **games** there are three more nodes, all fixed by the design: $Z$, the shown partner attributes (age, sex, religion, class, EU stance shown or not); $G$, the game (dictator or trust); and $K$, the round.
$Z$ is randomized. $G$ and $K$ are design factors. All three only affect $Y$.

The arrow $T \to R$ in the games is a design arrow.
The partner's party was drawn with reference to the attachment answer, so explicit partisans meet a co-partisan in about half of the rounds and vote-anchored partisans in about one in ten (notebook 3.1, *How the Partner's Party Was Drawn*).
Within a type, $R$ is random.
This is why AP is always computed *within* type: conditioning on $T$ closes the only back door into $R$.

# Estimand

For a type $t \in \{\text{explicit}, \text{vote-anchored}\}$:

$$
AP_t = E(Y \mid Co, t) - E(Y \mid Out, t)
$$

$$
\Delta AP = AP_{\text{explicit}} - AP_{\text{vote-anchored}}
$$

The expectations are marginal means over parties, so they need a stated average.
They are computed by g-computation from the fitted model:

1. Take the observed rows of the anchor parties in the estimand population (below).
2. Set type and relation counterfactually to each of the four cells (explicit/vote-anchored × co/out), keeping everything else as observed, and predict the expected outcome for every row with all varying effects (country, party, person) included.
3. Average the predictions with **respondent weights**: every respondent-wave counts once, whatever their number of rows. Each anchor party then weighs by its number of respondents of both types together. The weight is the same in all four cells, so $\Delta AP$ is not driven by how types are distributed across parties.
4. Take differences draw by draw.

**Common support.** The type contrast within a party exists only where the party has respondents of both types.
The models are fitted on **all** anchor parties: single-type parties still inform the country and grand means through partial pooling.
The estimands are averaged only over anchor parties with at least one explicit **and** at least one vote-anchored respondent.
The data sections tabulate per country what falls outside.

The estimands are reported pooled (all parties in support), per country and per anchor party.
They are reported in raw units (tokens, thermometer points) and in SD units: the raw contrast divided by the SD of the outcome in its analysis sample.
The SD units make the attitudinal and behavioral contrast comparable.
The cross-outcome quantity is

$$
\Delta AP^{SD}_{\text{thermo}} - \Delta AP^{SD}_{\text{games}},
$$

reported with the posterior probability that it is positive.
The two models are fitted separately, so draws are paired by index, which is valid for independent posteriors.
This quantity is coded here, but not interpreted.

The estimand code is shared by all models:

- `ap_draws()` returns posterior draws of the four cell means per group.
- `ap_contrasts()` turns them into $AP_t$ and $\Delta AP$.
- `summarise_ap()` gives the posterior median and the 95% interval.

`posterior_epred()` is called in row chunks, so that the full samples fit into memory.

```{r}
#| label: estimand-code

# Cell means per group, by draw
ap_draws <- function(
  fit,
  data,
  by = NULL,
  ndraws = 1000,
  back = identity,
  chunk = 5000
) {
  set.seed(seed)
  ids <- sample(seq_len(ndraws(fit)), min(ndraws, ndraws(fit)))
  grp <- factor(if (is.null(by)) "pooled" else data[[by]])

  # Respondent weights per group
  m <- sapply(levels(grp), \(l) (grp == l) * data$der_w)
  m <- matrix(m, nrow = nrow(data), dimnames = list(NULL, levels(grp)))
  rows <- split(seq_len(nrow(data)), ceiling(seq_len(nrow(data)) / chunk))

  cells <- expand_grid(der_explicit = 0:1, der_co = 0:1)
  map2(cells$der_explicit, cells$der_co, \(e, k) {
    nd <- mutate(data, der_explicit = e, der_co = k)
    tot <- 0
    for (i in rows) {
      ep <- back(posterior_epred(fit, newdata = nd[i, ], draw_ids = ids))
      tot <- tot + ep %*% m[i, , drop = FALSE]
    }
    tot <- sweep(tot, 2, colSums(m), "/")
    as_tibble(tot) |>
      mutate(.draw = ids) |>
      pivot_longer(-.draw, names_to = "group", values_to = "mean") |>
      mutate(der_explicit = e, der_co = k)
  }) |>
    bind_rows()
}

# AP by type and Delta AP, by draw
ap_contrasts <- function(draws, sd_y) {
  draws |>
    pivot_wider(
      names_from = c(der_explicit, der_co),
      values_from = mean,
      names_prefix = "m"
    ) |>
    transmute(
      .draw,
      group,
      ap_vote_anchored = m0_1 - m0_0,
      ap_explicit = m1_1 - m1_0,
      delta_ap = ap_explicit - ap_vote_anchored,
      delta_ap_sd = delta_ap / sd_y
    )
}

# Median and 95% interval
summarise_ap <- function(contrasts) {
  contrasts |>
    pivot_longer(-c(.draw, group), names_to = "quantity") |>
    summarise(
      median = median(value),
      lower = quantile(value, 0.025),
      upper = quantile(value, 0.975),
      .by = c(group, quantity)
    )
}
```

The MCMC diagnostics are also shared.
`mcmc_check()` reports the largest $\hat{R}$, the smallest bulk and tail ESS over all parameters, the number of divergent transitions, and the number of iterations that hit the maximum tree depth.
One `posterior` warning is muted: "The ESS has been capped to avoid unstable estimates". It fires once per parameter whose ESS estimate exceeds what the number of draws can support. With thousands of person intercepts it would otherwise flood the output, and it never signals a sampling problem. All other warnings stay visible.

```{r}
#| label: diagnostics-code

mcmc_check <- function(fit) {
  # Mute only the ESS-cap note
  s <- withCallingHandlers(
    summarise_draws(as_draws_df(fit), "rhat", "ess_bulk", "ess_tail"),
    warning = \(w) {
      if (grepl("ESS has been capped", conditionMessage(w))) {
        invokeRestart("muffleWarning")
      }
    }
  )
  np <- nuts_params(fit)
  tibble(
    max_rhat = max(s$rhat, na.rm = TRUE),
    min_ess_bulk = min(s$ess_bulk, na.rm = TRUE),
    min_ess_tail = min(s$ess_tail, na.rm = TRUE),
    divergent = sum(np$Value[np$Parameter == "divergent__"]),
    max_treedepth = sum(
      np$Value[np$Parameter == "treedepth__"] >= max_treedepth
    )
  )
}
```

# Formal Models

All four models share one structure.
For observation $i$ of person $j$, whose anchor party $p$ lies in country $c$:

$$
\eta_i = \alpha + \beta_R R_i + \beta_T T_i + \beta_{RT} R_i T_i + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{z}_i^\top (\mathbf{u}_c + \mathbf{u}_{cp}) + v_j,
$$

where:

- $R_i$ is `der_co` and $T_i$ is `der_explicit`;
- $\mathbf{x}_i$ are the controls (games: game, round, wave and the shown partner attributes; thermometer: wave);
- $\mathbf{z}_i = (1, R_i, T_i, R_i T_i)$;
- $\mathbf{u}_c \sim \text{MVN}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma_C)$ and $\mathbf{u}_{cp} \sim \text{MVN}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma_P)$ are the country and party deviations in intercept, co/out gap, type gap and their interaction;
- $v_j \sim N(0, \sigma_v)$ is a person intercept that spans both waves of a panelist.

The party-level slopes let the type × relation contrast differ between parties, so it is estimated *within* party. Countries differ through $\mathbf{u}_c$ and through the parties they hold.
The game and the round are controls only: they shift the level, and they do not vary by party or country.

The two families:

- **Beta-binomial** (the generative model): $Y_i \sim \text{BetaBinomial}(n, \mu_i, \phi)$ with $\text{logit}(\mu_i) = \eta_i$, $n = 10$ tokens or $n = 100$ thermometer points. $E(Y_i) = n\mu_i$, and $\phi$ governs how much the person-specific propensity spreads beyond the binomial. A small $\phi$ allows the spikes at the scale ends.
- **Gaussian** (robustness): $Y^z_i \sim N(\eta_i, \sigma)$ on the z-scored outcome. It recovers conditional means, but it puts predictive mass outside the scale and cannot produce the spikes.

## Priors

The priors aim for two things:

- every coefficient is centred on zero, so that the prior expectation of every contrast is no difference;
- the implied outcomes spread across the logical range of the scale rather than piling up anywhere.

The prior predictive checks below test the second aim.

**Why these priors (history).**
A first set had wider priors: Intercept normal(0, 1.5), every b normal(0, 1), every SD exponential(1), and phi exponential(0.1); for the Gaussian, b normal(0, 0.5), SD exponential(2) and sigma exponential(1).
It produced U-shaped prior predictive distributions, rendered in commit `c48c1aa`.
In the games, the prior median share was 30% at 0 tokens and 26% at 10. In the Gaussian models, about 41--46% of the draws fell outside the scale.
The cause is additive. On the logit scale, the predictor of one row sums the intercept, up to 17 control dummies, four varying effects at both the country and the party level, and a person intercept. With wide priors on each, the sum spreads over many logits, and the inverse logit then pushes most of the mass to the bounds.
A small phi (mass near 0 under exponential(0.1)) adds a second U-shape inside every row.

The current set was chosen by simulating the prior predictive through the model's own design (same dummies, centred intercept, varying effects at country, party and person level) on the development subsample.
It keeps every coefficient centred on zero. It narrows the parts that only add spread (controls, varying-effect SDs, phi near 0), but not the three **focal terms**: co, explicit, and co × explicit.
These keep a wider normal(0, 1). The prior must not rule out a large co/out gap. On the thermometer, a gap of 2--3 logits means, for example, 85 against 35 points: a large difference, but one the scale is built to record. A tighter focal prior would put such a gap many prior SDs out and pull the contrast of interest towards zero.

In brms, the `Intercept` class is the intercept at the sample means of the predictors, so its prior centres the *average* prediction.

- **Beta-binomial (logit scale).**
  - Intercept normal(0, 0.75): centred on $\mu = 0.5$ (5 tokens, 50 points), 95% of the mass between about 0.19 and 0.81.
  - Focal b normal(0, 1): a gap of $\pm 2$ logits (0.5 to 0.12 or 0.88) remains well within the prior.
  - Control b normal(0, 0.25): game, round, wave and the shown partner attributes shift the level by at most about half a logit each.
  - sd exponential(5): mean 0.2 logit for every varying effect, with 5% of the mass above 0.6.
  - cor lkj(2): mild shrinkage of correlations towards zero.
  - phi gamma(4, 0.4): mean 10, SD 5. Less than 2% of the mass lies below 2, the region in which a beta distribution with mean 0.5 turns U-shaped. This rules out a U-shape *by prior*, but not by data: if the allocations call for a small phi, the posterior can still get there.
- **Gaussian (z-scale).**
  - Intercept normal(0, 0.5).
  - Focal b normal(0, 1): a gap of up to about 2 SD remains within the prior.
  - Control b normal(0, 0.2).
  - sd exponential(5): mean 0.2 SD.
  - sigma exponential(2): mean 0.5 SD.
  - cor lkj(2).

In the simulation, the beta-binomial prior predictive is nearly flat across the whole range: each token value takes 8.5--9.2%, with 10% at 0 and 9.5% at 10. The Gaussian prior predictive puts a median of 16% of draws outside the scale. Some of that is unavoidable for a model that ignores the bounds.

# Attitudinal vs. Behavioral

The cross-outcome quantity is the difference of the two $\Delta AP$ in SD units, from the beta-binomial models, with the posterior probability that it is positive.
The draws of the two independent fits are paired by index.
**The code runs here on development fits, and the output is not an estimate.**

```{r}
#| label: cross-outcome

cross <- inner_join(
  games_bb_ap |>
    transmute(i = row_number(), games = delta_ap_sd),
  thermo_bb_ap |>
    transmute(i = row_number(), thermo = delta_ap_sd),
  by = "i"
) |>
  mutate(diff = thermo - games)

cross |>
  summarise(
    median = median(diff),
    lower = quantile(diff, 0.025),
    upper = quantile(diff, 0.975),
    p_positive = mean(diff > 0)
  ) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
```

# Limitations and Open Points

- **The contrast is descriptive.** Unobserved traits ($U$) can drive both reporting an attachment and treating partisans differently. No respondent covariates are joined yet, and the conditions for a causal reading are open (`design.md`).
- **Thin vote-anchored co-partisan cells in the games.** Vote-anchored respondents met a co-partisan only by chance, about one round in ten, so many parties have few or no such rounds. Their party-level gap rests on partial pooling, and the intervals show it.
- **Out-partners differ by type.** Explicit partisans' out-partners were drawn from the other parties only, while vote-anchored respondents' partners came from all main parties. Both are "out", but from slightly different sets.
- **The Gaussian models are robustness.** They recover conditional means, but they put predictive mass outside the scales and cannot produce the spikes at the ends.
- **The vote is a European Parliament vote**, in wave 2 mostly an intended or hypothetical one (notebook 3.1).
- **Question order is unverified.** Whether the thermometers came before or after the games is not established (notebook 3.1).
- **The priors on the varying-effect SDs are fairly tight** (exponential(5), mean 0.2 on the logit or z scale). They were tightened to remove the U-shape of the first prior set (see [Priors](#priors)). With 25 countries and about 400 parties the data should dominate, but whether the posterior SDs press against the prior is worth checking after the full fit.
